[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fe/Word_embedding_illustration.svg/250px-Word_embedding_illustration.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Word_embedding_illustration.svg)Illustration of word embedding. Each word is a point in some space. Word embedding enables a processor to perform semantic operations like obtaining the capital of a given country.
In [natural language processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language_processing "Natural language processing"), a **word embedding** is a representation of a word. The [embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embedding_\(machine_learning\) "Embedding \(machine learning\)") is used in [text analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content_analysis "Content analysis"). Typically, the representation is a [real-valued](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number "Real number") vector that encodes the meaning of the word in such a way that the words that are closer in the vector space are expected to be similar in meaning.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-1) Word embeddings can be obtained using [language modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_model "Language model") and [feature learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feature_learning "Feature learning") techniques, where words or phrases from the vocabulary are mapped to [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_\(mathematics\) "Vector \(mathematics\)") of [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers "Real numbers"). 
Methods to generate this mapping include [neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_net_language_model "Neural net language model"),[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-2) [dimensionality reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionality_reduction "Dimensionality reduction") on the word [co-occurrence matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-occurrence_matrix "Co-occurrence matrix"),[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-3)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-4)[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-5) probabilistic models,[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-6) explainable knowledge base method,[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-7) and explicit representation in terms of the context in which words appear.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-8)
Word and phrase embeddings, when used as the underlying input representation, have been shown to boost the performance in NLP tasks such as [syntactic parsing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntactic_parsing "Syntactic parsing")[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-9) and [sentiment analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentiment_analysis "Sentiment analysis").[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-10)
## Development and history of the approach
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Word_embedding&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Development and history of the approach")]
In [distributional semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributional_semantics "Distributional semantics"), a quantitative methodological approach for understanding meaning in observed language, word embeddings or semantic [feature space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feature_space "Feature space") models have been used as a knowledge representation for some time.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-11) Such models aim to quantify and categorize semantic similarities between linguistic items based on their distributional properties in large samples of language data. The underlying idea that "a word is characterized by the company it keeps" was proposed in a 1957 article by [John Rupert Firth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._R._Firth "J. R. Firth"),[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-12) but also has roots in the contemporaneous work on search systems[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-13) and in cognitive psychology.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-14)
The notion of a semantic space with lexical items (words or multi-word terms) represented as vectors or embeddings is based on the computational challenges of capturing distributional characteristics and using them for practical application to measure similarity between words, phrases, or entire documents. The first generation of semantic space models is the [vector space model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_space_model "Vector space model") for information retrieval.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-Salton_original-15)[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-SaltonEA_CACM-16)[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-17) Such vector space models for words and their distributional data implemented in their simplest form results in a very sparse vector space of high dimensionality (cf. [curse of dimensionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse_of_dimensionality "Curse of dimensionality")). Reducing the number of dimensions using linear algebraic methods such as [singular value decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular-value_decomposition "Singular-value decomposition") then led to the introduction of [latent semantic analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latent_semantic_analysis "Latent semantic analysis") in the late 1980s and the [random indexing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_indexing "Random indexing") approach for collecting word co-occurrence contexts.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-18)[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-19)[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-20)[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-21) In 2000, [Bengio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshua_Bengio "Yoshua Bengio") et al. provided in a series of papers titled "Neural probabilistic language models" to reduce the high dimensionality of word representations in contexts by "learning a distributed representation for words".[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-22)[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-23)[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-24)
A study published in [NeurIPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NeurIPS "NeurIPS") (NIPS) 2002 introduced the use of both word and document embeddings applying the method of kernel [CCA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_correlation "Canonical correlation") to bilingual (and multi-lingual) corpora, also providing an early example of [self-supervised learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-supervised_learning "Self-supervised learning") of word embeddings.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-25)
Word embeddings come in two different styles, one in which words are expressed as vectors of co-occurring words, and another in which words are expressed as vectors of linguistic contexts in which the words occur; these different styles are studied in Lavelli et al., 2004.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-26) Roweis and Saul published in _[Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_\(journal\) "Science \(journal\)")_ how to use "[locally linear embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_dimensionality_reduction#Locally-linear_embedding "Nonlinear dimensionality reduction")" (LLE) to discover representations of high dimensional data structures.[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-27) Most new word embedding techniques after about 2005 rely on a [neural network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_network "Neural network") architecture instead of more probabilistic and algebraic models, after foundational work done by Yoshua Bengio[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-28)[_[circular reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Wikipedia_and_sources_that_mirror_or_use_it "Wikipedia:Verifiability")_] and colleagues.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-29)[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-30)
The approach has been adopted by many research groups after theoretical advances in 2010 had been made on the quality of vectors and the training speed of the model, as well as after hardware advances allowed for a broader [parameter space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameter_space "Parameter space") to be explored profitably. In 2013, a team at [Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google "Google") led by [Tomas Mikolov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomas_Mikolov "Tomas Mikolov") created [word2vec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word2vec "Word2vec"), a word embedding toolkit that can train vector space models faster than previous approaches. The word2vec approach has been widely used in experimentation and was instrumental in raising interest for word embeddings as a technology, moving the research strand out of specialised research into broader experimentation and eventually paving the way for practical application.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-31)
## Polysemy and homonymy
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Word_embedding&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Polysemy and homonymy")]
Historically, one of the main limitations of static word embeddings or word [vector space models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_space_model "Vector space model") is that words with multiple meanings are conflated into a single representation (a single vector in the semantic space). In other words, [polysemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysemy "Polysemy") and [homonymy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homonym "Homonym") are not handled properly. For example, in the sentence "The club I tried yesterday was great!", it is not clear if the term _club_ is related to the word sense of a _[club sandwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Club_sandwich "Club sandwich")_ , _[clubhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meeting_house "Meeting house")_ , _[golf club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golf_club "Golf club")_ , or any other sense that _club_ might have. The necessity to accommodate multiple meanings per word in different vectors (multi-sense embeddings) is the motivation for several contributions in NLP to split single-sense embeddings into multi-sense ones.[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-32)[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-33)
Most approaches that produce multi-sense embeddings can be divided into two main categories for their word sense representation, i.e., unsupervised and knowledge-based.[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-34) Based on [word2vec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word2vec "Word2vec") skip-gram, Multi-Sense Skip-Gram (MSSG)[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-35) performs word-sense discrimination and embedding simultaneously, improving its training time, while assuming a specific number of senses for each word. In the Non-Parametric Multi-Sense Skip-Gram (NP-MSSG) this number can vary depending on each word. Combining the prior knowledge of lexical databases (e.g., [WordNet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WordNet "WordNet"), [ConceptNet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Mind_Common_Sense "Open Mind Common Sense"), [BabelNet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BabelNet "BabelNet")), word embeddings and [word sense disambiguation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_sense_disambiguation "Word sense disambiguation"), Most Suitable Sense Annotation (MSSA)[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-36) labels word-senses through an unsupervised and knowledge-based approach, considering a word's context in a pre-defined sliding window. Once the words are disambiguated, they can be used in a standard word embeddings technique, so multi-sense embeddings are produced. MSSA architecture allows the disambiguation and annotation process to be performed recurrently in a self-improving manner.[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-37)
The use of multi-sense embeddings is known to improve performance in several NLP tasks, such as [part-of-speech tagging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Part-of-speech_tagging "Part-of-speech tagging"), semantic relation identification, [semantic relatedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_relatedness "Semantic relatedness"), [named entity recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Named_entity_recognition "Named entity recognition") and sentiment analysis.[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-:1-38)[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-39)
As of the late 2010s, contextually-meaningful embeddings such as [ELMo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ELMo "ELMo") and [BERT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BERT_\(language_model\) "BERT \(language model\)") have been developed.[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-40) Unlike static word embeddings, these embeddings are at the token-level, in that each occurrence of a word has its own embedding. These embeddings better reflect the multi-sense nature of words, because occurrences of a word in similar contexts are situated in similar regions of BERT's embedding space.[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-41)[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-42)
## For biological sequences: BioVectors
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Word_embedding&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: For biological sequences: BioVectors")]
Word embeddings for _n-_ grams in biological sequences (e.g. [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA"), [RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA "RNA"), and [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proteins "Proteins")) for [bioinformatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioinformatics "Bioinformatics") applications have been proposed by Asgari and Mofrad.[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-:0-43) Named bio-vectors (BioVec) to refer to biological sequences in general with protein-vectors (ProtVec) for proteins (amino-acid sequences) and gene-vectors (GeneVec) for gene sequences, this representation can be widely used in applications of deep learning in [proteomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proteomics "Proteomics") and [genomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genomics "Genomics"). The results presented by Asgari and Mofrad[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-:0-43) suggest that BioVectors can characterize biological sequences in terms of biochemical and biophysical interpretations of the underlying patterns. 
## Game design
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Word_embedding&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Game design")]
Word embeddings with applications in [game design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_design "Game design") have been proposed by Rabii and Cook[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-:2-44) as a way to discover [emergent gameplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergent_gameplay "Emergent gameplay") using logs of gameplay data. The process requires transcribing actions that occur during a game within a [formal language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_language "Formal language") and then using the resulting text to create word embeddings. The results presented by Rabii and Cook[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-:2-44) suggest that the resulting vectors can capture expert knowledge about games like [chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess "Chess") that are not explicitly stated in the game's rules. 
## Sentence embeddings
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Word_embedding&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Sentence embeddings")]
Main article: [Sentence embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence_embedding "Sentence embedding")
The idea has been extended to embeddings of entire sentences or even documents, e.g. in the form of the [thought vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought_vector "Thought vector") concept. In 2015, some researchers suggested "skip-thought vectors" as a means to improve the quality of [machine translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_translation "Machine translation").[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-45) A more recent and popular approach for representing sentences is Sentence-BERT, or SentenceTransformers, which modifies pre-trained [BERT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BERT_\(language_model\) "BERT \(language model\)") with the use of siamese and triplet network structures.[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-46)

## Ethical implications
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Word_embedding&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Ethical implications")]
Word embeddings may contain the biases and stereotypes contained in the trained dataset, as Bolukbasi et al. points out in the 2016 paper "Man is to Computer Programmer as Woman is to Homemaker? Debiasing Word Embeddings" that a publicly available (and popular) word2vec embedding trained on [Google News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_News "Google News") texts (a commonly used data corpus), which consists of text written by professional journalists, still shows disproportionate word associations reflecting gender and racial biases when extracting word analogies.[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-56) For example, one of the analogies generated using the aforementioned word embedding is "man is to computer programmer as woman is to homemaker".[[57]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-57)[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-58)
Research done by Jieyu Zhou et al. shows that the applications of these trained word embeddings without careful oversight likely perpetuates existing bias in society, which is introduced through unaltered training data. Furthermore, word embeddings can even amplify these biases.[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-59)[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_embedding#cite_note-60)
